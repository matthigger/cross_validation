Cross validation, visually

How well does an estimator do on samples it has never seen? To quantify this, we'll need to observe its performance on “new” (to the estimator, at least) samples.

k-fold cross validation

Goal: estimate how well the estimator performs on new samples. A truly new sample was never seen in training, so we never test the estimator on a sample it was trained on: that would give it an advantage it will not have in practice.

Split the samples into k equal-size groups, called folds. Each round holds out one fold for testing and trains the estimator on the other k−1 (training). After k rounds, every sample has been tested exactly once, by an estimator that never saw it.

Each penguin i has a label yi: y = 0 Adelie y = 1 Chinstrap y = 2 Gentoo and an estimate ŷi, one of the same three.
CV accuracy = 1n ∑i 1[ ŷi = yi ]
Each penguin i has a feature and a label: xi = flipper length (mm) yi = body mass (g) Its estimate ŷi comes from xi, and its error is yi − ŷi.
CV MSE = 1n ∑i (yi − ŷi)2

Here ŷi always comes from the round where sample i was testing.

Try this

  • Step through every round. Each penguin is tested exactly once, and its estimate stays fixed afterwards.
  • Switch to Sorted by species with k = 3. Each testing fold is a single species, and that species is missing from training. The estimator cannot output a species it never saw, so every estimate is wrong.
  • Switch back to Shuffled, set k = 2 and reshuffle a few times. Most orders work, but some leave a fold short of one species.
  • Choose Stratified: each fold gets the same mix of species (as nearly as k allows), so no reshuffle can leave a species out of training.
  • Watch the line from round to round. It shifts a little, because each round fits it to different penguins. A testing penguin far from its line gets a tall error bar.
  • Switch to Sorted by x or Sorted by y with k = 3. Why could sorting cause trouble, and what should you do about it?
    AnswerEach testing fold holds only the smallest, middle or largest values, so the line is fit on penguins unlike the ones it is tested on. It has to extrapolate, the errors in the end folds mostly share one sign, and the CV error comes out too large: at k = 3, an RMSE of roughly 470 to 500 g against about 350 g when shuffled. Shuffle the samples before splitting them into folds.
  • Set k = 12 (leave-one-out): twelve rounds, each testing one penguin, and the result no longer depends on the order. What is the downside? Going from k = 6 to k = 12, how many more penguins does each estimator train on, and how many more estimators are trained?
    AnswerEach estimator trains on n − n/k penguins: 10 at k = 6 and 11 at k = 12. That is one more penguin each, but 12 estimators instead of 6: twice the training time.
